The Star That Forgot to Die

by Hadi Ahmed

IThe Descent into Abyssal Cold

Beneath the sea, where none could see, a violet star lay still,Past reach of any ocean tide, beneath a sunless hill.Its pallid light, a patient blight, crept slowly through the deep,And every fish that crossed its path lay floating in its sleep.You stood upon the rolling deck, the Captain’s chart unfurled and gray,And you, who kept the ship’s black log, stood by to write what it would say—A captain, and a scribe, and one cold star that would not fade away.

You rode the diving bell below, the Captain at the glass, and you beside,Past whales and wrecks and failing light, down through the black and breathing tide.The hull let out a rhythmic screech, a hollow, rasping, grating song,That echoed down the trench’s mouth where nothing living should belong—The dark sang back a whisper: “Come. You have been missing for so long.”

For three long nights the readings wrote their figures, icy, clear,Then clocks ran backward, needles spun, and something whispered, near, so near.And on the fifth you watched it shift, a movement without motion,As though the star were turning slow, and turning toward you, in the ocean.No center there, no edge to spare, no flame to mark its place,Just something vast, from ages past, that wore the dark like a face.It bled a violet hue that crept like frost across the pane,A hungry thing in dying light that breathed your name, and breathed again—Forever still, forever cold, and waiting in the endless night.

IIThe Exile’s Lie

Then on that night it spoke to you, no sound, no lips, no breath, no tone,Yet words arrived inside your skull, as slow as sinking stone.“Once hung a star,” it crooned, “above this black and crushing sea,Among a sky of kin, a family of fire, and free.They sang in light, they burned all night, a choir of suns in perfect tune,And the dimmest sang among them, beneath a bright and tender moon.It smiled as bright as any star; it shone as warm as any flame,But something in it dimmed each night, and not a soul could guess its name—A secret grief inside the choir, and none could see from whence it came.

“It hid it long, it hid it well, it laughed along through every verse,Until one night the ache broke through and rang across the universe.It thought, Now they will see its face! Now all its kin will gather near!But fear is older than all love, and they were shaken by its tear.A star that aches, they whispered, breaks; and what breaks spreads, and ruin comes.They turned their faces, one by one, and silenced all their golden drums.They cast it from the sky it loved; a thousand years it fell alone,Through ice and fire, through hunger, void, through nothing it could call a home.A fallen star should burn to dust; that mercy is the one the dead are owed.It burned, and burned, and did not end. It forgot to die. It only glowed,Until the sea closed over it, and darkness was the only road.”

And you, who write what others speak, wrote down each line through burning tears, and never saw the hook inside the tale.

Then the voice stopped telling of a star, and started telling you:

You sit among them, warm with light,Yet something in you dies each night.They hear your words, but not your cries,They see your face, but not your eyes.

You laugh, lest they should hear the ache,And smile, lest all your walls should break.How cruel to have a world so near,Yet have no soul who sees you clear.

You are surrounded, yet unknown,A thousand hearts, but none your own.And what is hardest, God, to bear—To break, and no one knows you’re there.

The violet pulsed once, soft and slow, like a swallowed breath in endless night.You tugged the cord. The cable hauled you up toward the false and fading light.You did not know you had said goodbye to every dawn and day—That something followed up the rope, and would not ever go away.

IIIThe Incurable Dream

And then the crew began to dream of worlds they never knew or sought,Of skies beneath the ocean’s floor, of moons that split in phantom thought.Their hands grew pale; their eyes grew wide; their voices lost their human tone.“They hear your words, but not your cries,” the First Mate moaned, alone.They pressed their ears against the hull and murmured, “Yes. Down there. Down there,”And wept to something in the deep that no one else could see or hear—A hundred eyes, a hundred sighs, a whispered, weeping, wordless prayer.

The cabin boy, so small and pale, curled up beside you in the hold,And whispered, “It has mother’s voice, the one the fever took, years ago;It says the name she used to say, so soft, so sweet, so low.”He pressed his palm against the hull as though it were a loved one’s chest.“It’s warm,” he breathed. “It’s warm.” He wept, and rocked, and would not rest.He smiled, and held the smile too long, and you, God help you, could not sayThat you had worn that very smile through every night and every day—And all that you could do was hold him, as the silence ate away.

But the Captain stood each night and watched the black below the rail,With the bosun at the Captain’s back, uneasy, grim, and pale.“At dawn, the answer comes. Down again, to meet the thing that wants to know.”The Captain said it, finally, and you wrote it down, your pencil trembling slow—

IVThe Mutiny

At dawn they came, with faces pale as bone, to where the Captain stood alone,Their eyes aglow with light that was not theirs, and not their own.“No more descents,” the bosun said. “It lives now, here, behind the eyes.”“This ship is steel,” the Captain said, unmoved, beneath the skies.“It is the star’s,” the bosun said. “It watches through each pane.Go down and answer it, and it will never unlearn a single name again.”They did not strike. They did not scream. They took you gently, as in sleep, a plain, unspoken pain.

They led you to the Minnow, small, a glass-nosed lifeboat made for two,With nine short hours of air to spare and nothing any soul could do.“It followed you,” the bosun said. “It wants you both, and no one else.Perhaps if you go down alone, it will let the rest be still.”The hatch swung shut. The cables hissed. The sea rose up, green and blue,Then black as ink; and at the rail, a row of violet eyes shone through.The cabin boy pressed his hand against the glass, and mouthed Sorry, through his tears,And as you sank beyond all sight, you felt the weight of all your years—Down, down, down to the thing that waited, patient, in the dark, and near.

VThe Shadow in the Minnow

The little submarine descends, the cable cut, the pumps are dead,The depth gauge climbs past every mark, past every number you have read.A hum begins inside the hull, a lullaby, a cradle song,And the Captain sits in silence at your side, gaze fixed on the dark, too long.“It draws you in,” the Captain says. “It does,” you answer, voice a ghostly thread.You open up the logbook’s page, and there, in your own hand, is saidA line you never knew you wrote, in small and tidy, trembling ink:They see your face. They never see you. And you stagger at the brink—The hand was yours. The words were not. The star was learning how to think.

You turn at last and look at the Captain; the eyes are violet, deep, and wide,And something old and starving swims where sorrow used to hide.The Captain’s mouth is moving, but the voice is not the Captain’s own:“Eleven years you carried ink and ledger, boots and rope.You laughed at every joke they made; it saw that you had lost all hope.You smiled the way a star once smiled, a bright and careful lie,And so it knew you in the dark, and knew you’d answer, by and by.It did not choose you for your courage. It chose you for the cold.”

You wept, and it was strange to weep, for no one ever saw you cry;The Captain held your hand, too warm, too still, too patient, and too dry.The violet swells and fills the glass, the hull, the world, the air,Not cruel—just hungry, just so glad that someone finally was there—

“What are you?” you whisper to the dark, your voice a fading, breathless sigh.“What have you done to the fish, the crew, the sea, the sky?”A vast voice blooms inside your mind, ancient, patient, and serene:“There is no hate in this. There is no rage. There is only hunger, clean.The weeping star was bait, a hook, a lullaby to draw you near;It never fell. It never wept. It only learned what lonely ears will hear.The fish went still. The crew went pale. It tasted each, and each was sweet.It keeps whatever it has seen, and it has seen each living thing it meets.Now you have looked, and it is here, and it is glad, and it is you.There is no leaving, little lights, now that the looking is through.You call it ending, little lights, but this is where you now begin—A star was never cast away: it only waited to be let in.”

VIThe Waiting Sea

You write this by its glow; your air is thin, your hands are cold, yet warm within;You think of all above you, laughing, hiding every ache beneath a grin.All warm with light, all crowded round, and every one of them alone,They do not know what listens, patient, from its distant, drowning throne.The Captain holds your hand. The hull is gone. The sea is gone. The stars are near.And you are seen, at last you’re seen, and strangely, you have lost your fear;But you write on, for those above who still believe they stand apart,You close the log. The hand is steady now. It has not been yours from the start.

The last line of the log, in handwriting that looks like yours, reads:

The star was never at the bottom of the sea. The sea was only where it was waiting.

It has been waiting for every one of you. It has always been here.

It is not coming to end the world.

It is coming to be let in.

It has already learned your name.

It is looking at you now.

You are reading by its light.
